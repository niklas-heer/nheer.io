When I was somewhere around twelve, thirteen, or fourteen, my friends and I had an idea: a racing game with overhead projectors.

The age is approximate. The vehicle choice, unfortunately, was quite deliberate.

I never got around to making it. The idea stayed in the back of my mind, occasionally resurfacing without bringing a finished game—or any 3D-modeling skills—with it. I still do not have those skills today.

Then GPT-6 Astra arrived, and I wanted to see what I could do with it. This seemed like a good test: could I finally turn that old idea into something playable?

![Overhead Overdrive's courtyard menu, with a classroom projector waiting at the starting line](/assets/articles/2026/projector-courtyard.png)

The first attempt gave me surprisingly nice results quite quickly. Enough that my reaction was essentially: “Hey, why not?”

So I kept going.

The result is [Overhead Overdrive](https://github.com/niklas-heer/overhead-overdrive): a browser racing game with a very questionable choice of vehicle. The screenshots here come from the first playable version and its early polish, rather than a mockup of what it might become.

![An actual race in the school atrium, with several overhead projectors crowding into a corner](/assets/articles/2026/projector-race.png)

I could give you a detailed explanation of the drift system. But the part I find most interesting is much simpler: an idea that had lived in my head since school now exists as something I can play.

That does not mean I suddenly became a 3D artist. It means I had a way to try the idea without first becoming one.

Sometimes a new tool is useful because it saves time on work you already do. Sometimes it gets you to attempt the slightly ridiculous thing you have been postponing for years.

In this case, the slightly ridiculous thing has wheels and used to explain fractions.
